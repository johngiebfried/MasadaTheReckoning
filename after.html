After MasadaHow the story was remembered.

For nineteen centuries, everything anyone knew about Masada came from one book. This is what different ages did with it. It takes about five minutes.

Content note: this page mentions mass suicide and the killing of children.

Begin

Rome, the late 70s CE

One book.

Josephus finished The Jewish War in Rome, under the patronage of Vespasian and Titus. He says he gave them copies, and that Titus signed the books with his own hand and ordered them published. It was the official history of the war that had made the Flavians emperors, and it was written for Roman readers. Rome had paraded the spoils of the Temple through the city and struck coins reading Judaea Capta, “Judaea captured.” Josephus’s book told the same victory at length.

Masada comes near the end, as the last fortress of the war. Josephus gives its commander, Eleazar son of Yair, two long speeches on freedom, death and the soul. The men he had earlier called brigands die with dignity, and the scene ends with the Romans, who found the bodies, unable to do anything but marvel at their resolve. Josephus never says whether they were right. He says the account came from a woman who hid in the cisterns.

The book survived because Christians copied it. Church writers read Josephus as a witness to the fall of Jerusalem, and monasteries kept copying him in Greek and Latin for a thousand years. Jews did not need his book to remember the war. They had their own way of remembering it.

The rabbis

The Talmud remembers the war.

The rabbis told many stories about the revolt. They told how a feud between two men, Kamtza and Bar Kamtza, brought the Romans down on Jerusalem. They told how the rebels in the city burned the grain stores to force the people to fight.

They told how Rabban Yohanan ben Zakkai had his students carry him out of the besieged city in a coffin, greeted Vespasian as king before the news came from Rome, and asked for one thing in return: Yavneh and its sages. It is the same prophecy Josephus claimed for himself. The rabbis gave it to their own man.

Masada is not there.

Not in the Mishnah, not in either Talmud, not in the midrash.

The Rhineland, 1096

The tradition knew how to remember a death like Masada. In the spring of 1096, crusaders marching east attacked the Jewish communities of the Rhineland. In Worms and Mainz, hundreds of Jews killed their children and themselves rather than be baptized.

The survivors wrote chronicles of what happened. They entered the names of the dead in memorial books read aloud in synagogue. They wrote dirges that are still recited on the Ninth of Av. They remembered the dead as martyrs who sanctified God’s name by refusing to abandon their faith.

By then the rabbis had settled when a Jew must accept death: rather than commit idolatry, sexual sin or murder, and in all other cases live. The martyrs of 1096 died to avoid baptism, and that fit the rule. The defenders of Masada died rather than be enslaved. For eighteen hundred years, the tradition that honored the martyrs of 1096 left Masada unspoken.

1927

The story came back with Zionism. In 1927 Yitzhak Lamdan, a young immigrant from Ukraine, published a long Hebrew poem called Masada. In it the rock stands for the Land of Israel, the last refuge of a people with nowhere else to go. One line became a slogan.

“Masada shall not fall again.”

1940s

Youth movement guides, above all Shmaria Guttman, led teenagers on long marches across the desert and up the rock. The Palmach, the strike force of the Jewish underground, made the climb a test. After 1948 the army took it up. For decades, recruits to the Armored Corps climbed at night and swore their oath on the summit with Lamdan’s line.

1963–1965

Yigael Yadin, archaeologist and former chief of staff of the army, excavated the rock with thousands of volunteers from Israel and abroad. He found Herod’s palaces, storerooms, a synagogue, ritual baths, scroll fragments, and a set of potsherds inscribed with names, one of them “ben Yair.” Yadin suggested they might be the lots of the last night. His book, Masada: Herod’s Fortress and the Zealots’ Last Stand, became a bestseller in several languages and presented the defenders as freedom fighters whose stand foreshadowed the new state.

7 July 1969

The state buried the human remains found in the excavation below the rock, with a full military ceremony.

The rock today.

The story traveled beyond Israel. In 1971 the American journalist Stewart Alsop coined the phrase “Masada complex” for what he saw as Israel’s siege mentality. In 1981 American television broadcast a miniseries, Masada, with Peter O’Toole as the Roman governor Silva.

From the 1990s, historians and sociologists, many of them Israeli, began to point out what the national story had left out: the massacre the Sicarii carried out at Ein Gedi, which Josephus records; the rabbis’ silence; and how little of the excavation matched the tale told on the summit. The best known of these studies, Nachman Ben-Yehuda’s The Masada Myth (1995), called the national story a myth built in the twentieth century. Others answered that every nation chooses what to remember. The argument over what Masada means has not been settled.

None of this has emptied the mountain. A cable car has run to the summit since 1971. In 2001 UNESCO named Masada a World Heritage Site. It is one of the most visited places in Israel. Every morning before dawn, people climb the Snake Path up the eastern face to watch the sun rise over the Dead Sea and the mountains of Jordan.

Almost all of them know the story. Almost all of what they know still comes from Josephus.
