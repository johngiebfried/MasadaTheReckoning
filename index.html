The Road to Masada
67 CEJotapata

The Road to MasadaRead this before we play.

In the spring of 73 CE, 960 people died on a rock above the Dead Sea. Almost everything we know about it comes from one man. This is how both of them got there. It takes about ten minutes.

Content note: this story includes mass suicide, the killing of children, crucifixion, and massacre. There is a note at the end on how we handle that in class.

Begin

Galilee, summer 67 CE

Jotapata.

For forty-seven days a Roman army besieged the hill town of Jotapata. Its commander was a priest from Jerusalem, not yet thirty, named Joseph son of Matthias. The Romans built a ramp and brought up rams. The defenders poured boiling oil on the men below. On the forty-seventh day, before dawn and in a fog, the Romans came over the wall.

The cave

Joseph and forty men hid in a cave. When the Romans found them, they offered Joseph his life. He wanted to accept. The others drew their swords and said they would kill him first.

Joseph proposed a way out. They would draw lots, and each man would be killed by the next, so that no one had to kill himself. They agreed. One by one, the men in the cave were killed.

Two men were left.

One of them was Joseph. He persuaded the other to surrender with him.

Josephus says the lots fell that way by chance, or by the providence of God. He never explained it further. Jewish War 3.387–391.

Brought before the Roman general Vespasian, he said

“You will be Caesar.”

Jewish War 3.399–402.

69 CE

Vespasian kept him in chains. Two years later the legions proclaimed Vespasian emperor, and he had Joseph set free. Joseph took the emperor’s family name. From then on he was Flavius Josephus.

Why we know any of this

Josephus spent the rest of the war on the Roman side. Afterward he lived in Rome on the emperor’s pension and wrote the history of the war, The Jewish War. Rome put down many revolts, and almost none left a record from the losing side. Between 6 and 9 CE a rebellion in Illyricum, across the Adriatic from Italy, tied down ten legions at once, and almost no one remembers it, because none of the rebels lived to write it down. The Jewish revolt is the exception. One of the men who fought Rome survived and wrote the whole war, and his book is the only full account we have. He wrote it under the protection of the family that won.

How did a young priest end up commanding an army against Rome? Go back a century.

63 BCE

In 63 BCE the Roman general Pompey took Jerusalem and walked into the inner room of the Temple, where only the high priest was allowed. He took nothing, but the Jews did not forget that he went in. Rome then let the Jews keep their Temple and their Law. Other provinces worshiped the emperor. In Jerusalem the priests offered a daily sacrifice for him instead. That arrangement held until 66 CE.

40–4 BCE

Herod the Great.

Rome made Herod king of the Jews. He ruled for thirty-three years with informers, a ban on public meetings, and quick death for rivals. He rebuilt the Temple on a doubled platform. In the desert he built fortress-palaces, among them one on a flat rock above the Dead Sea called Masada. Josephus says Herod built it as a refuge in case his own people rose against him.

Herod trusted no one in his own family. He executed the wife he loved most, Mariamne, along with her brother, grandfather and mother, and had two of their sons strangled.

The emperor Augustus is said to have remarked

“It is better to be Herod’s pig than his son.”

Macrobius, Saturnalia 2.4.11. In Greek it is a pun: hys, pig; huios, son. Herod kept kosher, so his pig was safe.

4 BCE

As Herod lay dying, two teachers and their students tore down the golden Roman eagle he had set over the Temple gate. He had them burned alive. Then he died, and none of his sons could hold Judaea.

6 CE

No king between Rome and the Temple.

In 6 CE Rome took over Judaea directly and counted its people for taxes. A Galilean named Judas told the Jews not to pay, because God alone was their master. Rome crushed him. His idea survived.

The governors Rome sent were men of the second rank, with troops recruited from the Greek cities of the coast, who hated the Jews. Pontius Pilate raided the Temple treasury for an aqueduct and clubbed the crowd that protested. In Josephus’s telling, each governor after him was worse than the last. Prophets gathered crowds and promised that God was about to act. Rome had one answer for all of them.

THE FACTIONS

Four answers to one question.

Josephus says the Jews of his day had four schools of thought. All agreed on one God and one Law. They disagreed about how to live under Rome. Your role sheet puts you inside one of them, or outside all of them.

PRIESTS AND LAND

Sadducees

The Temple aristocracy. They ran the Temple and kept terms with Rome. In a revolt they had the most to lose.

TEACHERS AND VILLAGES

Pharisees

Teachers, not priests, popular with ordinary people. They held the oral tradition equal to the written Law, and that the Law could be kept under any ruler. In the war they split: some fought, some refused. Josephus was one.

THE WITHDRAWN

Essenes

Separatists who thought the Temple corrupt and waited for God to end the age. Rome destroyed their settlement by the Dead Sea early in the war.

NO NAME

The fourth philosophy

Founded by Judas the Galilean. Paying tribute to Rome is slavery, and God alone is master. Josephus traces every rebel he dislikes back to it.

DAGGER MEN

Sicarii

Heirs of Judas the Galilean. From the 50s CE they stabbed their enemies in festival crowds. Their first victim was the former high priest Jonathan, and most of their victims were Jews. This game is about them.

THE WARTIME FACTION

Zealots

A different group: lower priests who seized the Temple during the war. They were no friends of the Sicarii. Many textbooks call every rebel “a Zealot.” Josephus does not, and this game does not.

AND SOME OF THE WITNESSES ARE NONE OF THESE

Samaritans worship on their own mountain and say Jerusalem has the wrong one. Nabatean traders sold to both sides. Roman soldiers and a Roman priest came with the legion. The account Josephus writes will say “the Jews,” and some of the people in the tent will object to being swallowed by that word.

Caesarea, spring 66

In Caesarea, Jews and Greeks had fought for years over whose city it was. One Sabbath a Greek set an upturned pot at the door of the synagogue and sacrificed birds on it. In the fighting that followed, the Jews left the city with their Torah scrolls. They had paid the governor, Gessius Florus, eight talents to protect them. He took the money and did nothing.

Jerusalem, spring 66

Then Florus took seventeen talents from the Temple treasury. When the crowd mocked him, he sent in his soldiers, and Josephus says 3,600 people died. A pro-Roman king begged the city to stop and was stoned out of it. The priests ended the daily sacrifice for the emperor.

That meant war.

The Sicarii

A Sicarii leader named Menahem, a descendant of Judas, seized Masada and its armory, then marched into Jerusalem like a king. His men killed the former high priest Ananias. Other rebels killed Menahem. His followers fled back to Masada under his kinsman Eleazar son of Yair. They never returned to Jerusalem.

That autumn the rebels routed a Roman legion in the pass at Beth Horon. Rome would come back with a real army.

67–69

Rome sent Vespasian with three legions, the Tenth among them. Josephus held Galilee for a year. You have seen how that ended. By the end of 67 Galilee was Roman again.

Jerusalem fought itself.

The Zealots seized the Temple. Their allies from Idumaea killed thousands in the city, among them the former high priest Ananus. Two warlords, John of Gischala and Simon bar Giora, took over other parts of the city. Three armies fought one another inside the walls.

In one of their fights they burned the city’s grain stores, enough to last through years of siege. Meanwhile Nero died, and in 69 Vespasian became emperor, as Josephus had said. He left the war to his son Titus.

Passover, 70

Titus.

Titus reached Jerusalem at Passover, with the city full of pilgrims, and walled it in. Starvation followed. Rome crucified captives before the walls, hundreds a day. Josephus walked below the walls calling on the defenders to surrender. They threw stones at him.

August–September 70

The Temple burned. Josephus says Titus tried to save it. Historians still argue about that. A month later the last of the city fell. Simon bar Giora hid underground until the food ran out, then rose out of the rubble where the Temple had stood, in a white tunic and a purple cloak, and gave himself up. Titus paraded him through Rome in chains and had him executed.

IVDAEA CAPTA.

71–72 CE

Three fortresses still held.

Herodium, Herod’s hill and tomb south of Bethlehem, surrendered.

Machaerus, east of the Dead Sea, held out until the Romans captured a young fighter and set up a cross for him in sight of the walls. The garrison surrendered on condition that they all leave alive, and the Romans kept the terms. The people of the town below were not part of the bargain. The Romans killed 1,700 of their men and enslaved the women and children.

That left one

Masada.

Drawn for this guide. Luyken made no plate of Masada.

66–73 CE

Herod’s rock.

Masada rises about four hundred meters above the Dead Sea, with cliffs on every side. Herod gave it palaces, storehouses, rock-cut cisterns and a wall around the summit. The Sicarii found his stores still full. Under Eleazar son of Yair they held the rock for the rest of the war and lived off the country below it. Josephus says that by the end there were 960 people there, men, women and children.

72 CE

The governor Flavius Silva came with the Tenth Legion. He walled in the whole mountain and built eight camps along the wall.

On a rocky spur against the western face, Josephus says, the Romans raised a ramp two hundred cubits high, about ninety meters. They rolled up an iron-plated siege tower, and a ram broke the wall. A later tradition says Jewish prisoners were made to build the ramp. Josephus does not say.

Spring 73

The defenders had built a second wall of timber and earth. The Romans set it on fire. The wind blew the flames back at them, then turned and burned the wall down. The Romans went back to camp. They would go in at first light.

That night

Eleazar told the men that in the morning they would be slaves, and that they could still choose to die free. Not all of them agreed. He spoke again, at length, and this time they did not refuse.

Each man killed his own wife and children. They burned their possessions but left the food, so the Romans would know they had not starved. Ten men chosen by lot killed the rest. One of the ten killed the other nine, set fire to the palace, and killed himself.

960.

The Romans came over the wall at dawn, armed for a fight, and found no one.

Then two women came out of the underground water channels where they had hidden with five children. One of them, a relative of Eleazar, told the Romans what had happened. At first they did not believe her.

Everything Josephus wrote about that night came, in the end, from those women. No one else who was there left a word.

Now you write it.

Josephus was not at Masada. By 73 he was in Rome. He never says how he learned what happened on the rock.

In this game, the witnesses come to him. It is autumn 73, in the tent of the governor, Flavius Silva. Six years ago Josephus walked out of a cave where forty men had drawn lots. Now he must write about 960 people who drew lots and did not walk out. What he writes will be the only account that survives.

The witnesses in the tent were on the rock, below it, around it, or in Jerusalem. Some of them know things this introduction did not tell you.

Silva owns the tent.

He decides who speaks, can silence anyone, and can refuse the whole account.

Josephus owns the page.

Winning the room is worth nothing if his sentence does not say your thing.

In front of Josephus is the Scroll: three questions with lettered answers and five lines he writes in his own words. Your role sheet names three things you want on it. That is all you are scored on, and nobody else will look after them for you.

First, testimony, by where you were when it happened. Then the argument, over the entries Josephus cannot decide alone. At the end he reads the Scroll aloud and everyone votes. Approve if all three of your things are on the page, Accept if two are, Reject if one or none. Whether your point is really in his sentence is your judgment, not his.

A NOTE BEFORE WE START

This story contains mass suicide, parents killing their children, the killing of civilians, crucifixion, and slavery. Some of you will argue positions you find repellent. Making a case is not holding it.

If you need to step out, say “I need a moment” and go. No explanation is needed. Your character goes quiet and the game keeps running.
